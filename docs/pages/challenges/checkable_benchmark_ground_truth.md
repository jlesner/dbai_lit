# Benchmarks with checkable ground truth

> Equivalence benchmarks whose labels can't be checked cap progress. A refutation benchmark's ground truth grows as models improve.

<p class="tags"><a class="tag" href="#/tags/judge">judge</a><a class="tag" href="#/tags/labels">labels</a><a class="tag" href="#/tags/pairs">pairs</a></p>

## Challenge
Benchmark labels usually can't be checked by the user: they come from a generator trusted to preserve meaning, from one test database, or from a hand judgment. Build benchmarks whose answers are [certificates](#/glossary/certificate), so scores and training rewards can be trusted, and whose ground truth grows as models improve.

## What's known
- **Equivalence benchmarks rest on trusted tools or one database.** EquiBench's "equivalent" labels come from the tools that made the pairs (compiler passes, a superoptimizer, online-judge verdicts), whose bugs and wrong verdicts, its authors concede, can mislabel pairs ([EquiBench](#/papers/wei2025equibench "EquiBench: Benchmarking Large Language Models' Reasoning about Program Semantics via Equivalence Checking (2025)") § Limitations). SQLEquiQuest's "equivalent" means identical results on one large database designed to cover corner cases ([Can the Rookies Cut…](#/papers/singh2024sqlequiquest "Can the Rookies Cut the Tough Cookie? Exploring the Use of LLMs for SQL Equivalence Checking (2024)") §3.1). SQL-RewriteBench checks its reference pairs, and gates the rewrites it scores, by result consistency on its packaged data, not formal equivalence ([SQL-RewriteBench](#/papers/long2026sqlrewritebench "SQL-RewriteBench: A Correctness-Gated, Full-Denominator Benchmark for Statement-Level SQL Rewriting (2026)") §4.1, §5.1), a check its authors say "does not prove that two queries are equivalent over every legal database instance" (§5.1); so its "unsafe" labels are certificates (the data separates the queries, ties aside) and its "consistent" ones are not (ours). UniQL accepts a translation automatically when its result matches the source's, an agreement its authors say can't fully guarantee equivalence ([UniQL](#/papers/gao2026uniql "UniQL: Towards Dialect-Universal Benchmarking for Text-to-SQL (2026)") §3.2, §8; home: [SQL dialect translation](#/challenges/dialect_translation) § What's known).
- **Refutation is a hard, checkable task beyond SQL.** The REFUTE authors propose "inverse benchmarks" that test falsifying an incorrect solution rather than solving the problem ([REFUTE ("Can Language Models Falsify?")](#/papers/sinha2025refute "Can Language Models Falsify? Evaluating Algorithmic Reasoning with Counterexample Creation (2025)") §1, §3), each counterexample checked by an input validator and against a correct solution (§3.2). Top reasoning models, they report, find counterexamples far less often than their ratings say they solve the problems, even given the correct solution (§5.1, Tab. 1; §6). UOJ-Bench checks each hack (an input that breaks a submission) with the problem's input validator and reference solution ([UOJ-Bench ("Beyond Problem Solving")](#/papers/xu2026uojbench "Beyond Problem Solving: UOJ-Bench for Evaluating Code Generation, Hacking, and Repair in Competitive Programming (2026)") §3.1); a hack on a submission passing every known test becomes a new test that re-judges all accepted submissions, a true bug when the reference passes it (§5, App. B.7): ground truth that grows as models find bugs, while the reference is right (ours). The checker needs checking too: REFUTE's released evaluator and SInQ's gpt-4o-mini harness ([Program Semantic Inequivalence Game…](#/papers/micelibarone2025sinq "Program Semantic Inequivalence Game with Large Language Models (2025)")) count some errors as counterexamples (ours, from their code; home: [Equivalence of nondeterministic queries](#/challenges/nondeterministic_equivalence) § Why it's hard).
- **Nearest work: counterexamples and certified labels.** Kikot et al. turn rows that separate two candidates into "evidence-based criticism of gold answers", though which is right is argued informally ([data-aware NL2SQL candidate selection](#/papers/kikot2026separating "Data-aware candidate selection in NL2SQL translation via small separating instances (2026)") §IV). On the accept side, a judge benchmark adds meaning-preserving rewrites labelled faithful, proved equivalent by Dafny's verifier or Lean [tactics](#/glossary/tactic) or argued per rule, so a judge can't score by spotting edits ([claimcheck](#/papers/amster2026claimcheck "claimcheck: Measuring and Improving Specification Faithfulness (2026)") §2). ModelEquivBench, by its authors' account, grades LLM-written optimization models against a reference on several relations, each decided one backed by independently re-checked evidence, each undecided one a typed unknown or N/A, not a guess ([ModelEquivBench](#/papers/zhu2026modelequivbench "ModelEquivBench: Certifying Multi-Relational Evaluation of LLM-Generated Optimization Models (2026)") abstract, §4.1). Where equivalence is decidable, grading can be exact (ours): Regel's authors write their StackOverflow gold in their regex DSL so an automaton library can check a tool's output equivalent to it ([Multi-modal Synthesis of Regular Expressions](#/papers/chen2019regel "Multi-modal Synthesis of Regular Expressions (2020)") App. D). Pairs that stay *unknown* are the open frontier: the Polygon authors call the pairs VeriEQL left unrefuted of "unknown" solvability, and checking them by hand "a non-starter" ([Polygon](#/papers/zhao2025polygon "Polygon: Symbolic Reasoning for SQL using Conflict-Driven Under-Approximation Search (2025)") §4). Judge-benchmark authors reach the same conclusion (our reading): EquiBench's list agents that execute and compare programs among future directions, after finding that "binary labels alone provide limited learning signals" for fine-tuning ([EquiBench](#/papers/wei2025equibench "EquiBench: Benchmarking Large Language Models' Reasoning about Program Semantics via Equivalence Checking (2025)") §6), and LLM-SQL-Solver's propose execution tools against the LLM's hallucinated runs ([LLM-SQL-Solver](#/papers/zhao2023llmsqlsolver "LLM-SQL-Solver: Can LLMs Determine SQL Equivalence? (2025)") §5.2).

## Why it matters
- **Scores and rankings rest on the labels.** The SpotIt authors report that searching for a database separating a BIRD prediction from its [gold query](#/glossary/gold-query), instead of comparing the two on the one test database, lowers every method's accuracy and reorders the ranking ([SpotIt](#/papers/klopfenstein2025spotit "SpotIt: Evaluating Text-to-SQL Evaluation with Formal Verification (2026)") §5, Tab. 3), though what survives its search, capped at five rows per table (§5), still counts as correct ([Few Rows Tell Them Apart](#/papers/cohen2026fewrows "Few Rows Tell Them Apart: Equivalence of Queries Mixing Set and Bag Semantics (2026)") §1). The SpotIt authors add that when the two disagree, "it is often the gold SQL that is incorrect" ([SpotIt](#/papers/klopfenstein2025spotit "SpotIt: Evaluating Text-to-SQL Evaluation with Formal Verification (2026)") §1), from a sample they labelled themselves (§5, Fig. 5). Re-judging by hand the Spider dev questions two top models both failed, Pourreza and Rafiei found many model queries correct and some gold queries wrong ([Evaluating Cross-Domain Text-to-SQL Models…](#/papers/pourreza2023evaluating "Evaluating Cross-Domain Text-to-SQL Models and Benchmarks (2023)") §6.2, Tab. 3); the judges were authors of one of the two models (§6.2 footnote), and their abstract's "relative performance changes" rests on that selected subset (ours). Correcting a sampled BIRD Dev subset reordered leaderboard agents ([Pervasive Annotation Errors Break…](#/papers/jin2026annotation "Pervasive Annotation Errors Break Text-to-SQL Benchmarks and Leaderboards (2026)") §5.3, §5.5), but answers also flip on uncorrected examples (Fig. 7): the changes mix corrections with run-to-run variance (ours). For agent-built data pipelines, ArrivalBench's author reports that a reimplementation of run-once grading gives near-identical, near-perfect scores to eleven models (one arm aside) whose correctness on re-delivered data differs widely, judged against a recomputation by an independent implementation so that "a gold that was itself wrong under a schedule could not certify itself" ([ArrivalBench](#/papers/kothari2026arrivalbench "ArrivalBench: Agent-Generated Data Pipelines Are Correct Once and Wrong Under Time (2026)") §4.2, App. C).
- **Training data and rewards inherit wrong labels.** The SQLDriller authors report that gold SQL in samples of Spider and BIRD training data is often wrong, though Spider's authors reviewed every mapping by hand ([SQLDriller](#/papers/yang2025sqldriller "Automated Validating and Fixing of Text-to-SQL Translation with Execution Consistency (2025)") abstract, §2.2, Tab. 1); its test-set corrections: [Checking text-to-SQL against intent](#/challenges/text_to_sql_verification) § Why it's hard. The ReViSQL authors, who corrected a BIRD Train sample, call pervasive annotation errors "the dominant bottleneck" for [RL with verifiable rewards](#/glossary/rl-with-verifiable-rewards-rlvr) on [text-to-SQL](#/glossary/text-to-sql) ([ReViSQL](#/papers/zhu2026revisql "Human-Level Text-to-SQL via Reinforcement Learning on Verified Data, Without Pipeline Engineering (2026)") abstract). A wrong label is a wrong reward, which an optimizer learns to hit ([Weak checkers get exploited](#/challenges/weak_checker_exploitation)).
- **Trusted sources mislabel too.** BIRD keeps a gold query when two annotators' independent queries return identical results on its database ([BIRD](#/papers/li2023bird "Can LLM Already Serve as A Database Interface? A BIg Bench for Large-Scale Database Grounded Text-to-SQLs (2023)") §3.4), which misses a shared misreading and any difference that database can't show (ours); an expert-checked audit still finds annotation errors, ambiguity included, in most of BIRD Mini-Dev ([Pervasive Annotation Errors Break…](#/papers/jin2026annotation "Pervasive Annotation Errors Break Text-to-SQL Benchmarks and Leaderboards (2026)") §3.2, §4.3; home: [Checking text-to-SQL against intent](#/challenges/text_to_sql_verification) § What's known). Two authors hand-checking BIRD's financial domain find wrong gold queries ([Understanding the Effects of…](#/papers/wretblad2024noise "Understanding the Effects of Noise in Text-to-SQL: An Examination of the BIRD-Bench Benchmark (2024)") §3.2, Tab. 1), and in one evaluation correcting them re-ranks prompting methods ([Comparing LLM methods under noise](#/challenges/method_comparison_under_noise) § What's known). SALUS, voting with LLM agents' SQL through rule-based labeling functions, estimates that a large share of BIRD's and Spider's development gold queries are wrong ([SALUS](#/papers/zhou2026salus "SALUS: Automated Auditing of NL-to-SQL Benchmarks through Weak Supervision of Multi-Agent Output (2026)") §5.4). On Spider-Realistic, a blinded expert audit supports an AI flag of suspected reference defects, which recur in families visible from running the reference alone ([Certified Against Which Oracle?](#/papers/liu2026whichoracle "Certified Against Which Oracle? Execution Labels Set the Reported Risk of Conformal Abstention for Text-to-SQL (2026)") §5.4, App. G). The VeriEQL authors found non-equivalent pairs among Calcite's rewrite tests, all meant to be equivalent (home: [Refuting faulty rewrite rules](#/challenges/faulty_rewrite_rules)), and LeetCode problems whose accepted submissions differ from the ground-truth query, all acknowledged by LeetCode and most fixed ([VeriEQL](#/papers/he2024verieql "VeriEQL: Bounded Equivalence Verification for Complex SQL Queries with Integrity Constraints (2024)") §6.3); VeriEQL's own bounded "checked" verdict, read as "equivalent", was wrong on some literature pairs inspected by hand (§6.2). In Python, ProbeGen's LLM with execution feedback disproves samples a benchmark's unit tests accept ([Disproving Program Equivalence with LLMs](#/papers/allamanis2025disproving "Disproving Program Equivalence with LLMs (2025)") §4.2; an estimate resting on an LLM filter).
- **Labels made by code or an engine run still need checking.** Procedural generators, scorers, LLM-written reference solvers and KGQA gold, even query-backed, can be wrong, and graph-query gold is checked only in part (homes: [A gym of DSL tasks with answer keys made by code](#/challenges/dsl_gym) § Why it's hard; dsl_benchmark_gold_errors § What's known); the Reasoning Core authors add that their retrospective audits of other libraries were "neither independent nor blinded" and are not aggregate judgments of them ([Reasoning Core](#/papers/sileo2026reasoningcore "Reasoning Core: Designing Broad Procedural Data for Completion-Supervised Reasoning Training (2026)") §3.4). Screening the verifiers of the agent benchmarks it reuses, the VerifierBench authors excluded tasks for confirmed defects, such as a test-output parser that counts skipped tests as passes ([VerifierBench](#/papers/yin2026verifierbench "VerifierBench: Benchmarking the Generation of Executable Verifiers for LLM Agents (2026)") §2.3). A scorer can also miss answers unevenly: out of domain, an extractor that reads only `\boxed{}` misses most of the base models' answers, stated in the text, and fewer of their RL successors', and the difference counts as a gain ([Same Answer, New Box](#/papers/lyu2026sameanswer "Same Answer, New Box: How Much of RLVR's Out-of-Domain Gain Is Answer Format? (2026)") §4.2; see [Gains made against a checker, carried beyond it](#/challenges/transfer_beyond_the_checker) § Why it's hard).
- **False accepts often cost more than false rejects** (ours). In [self-distillation](#/glossary/self-distillation) by count-matched rejection-sampling fine-tuning, held-out gain grows with the checker's precision while rejecting many correct outputs costs little; with [GRPO](#/glossary/grpo) in its place, false rejections push the policy away from [rollouts](#/glossary/rollout) that passed ([The Verifier is the Curriculum](#/papers/zhou2026curriculum "The Verifier is the Curriculum: Precision Sets the Return on Search in Code Self-Distillation (2026)") §8, Figs. 1, 5; weights trained). Random flips of test results at moderate rates barely hurt RL on code, though the study doesn't model persistent verifier blind spots ([An Imperfect Verifier is Good Enough](#/papers/plesner2026imperfect "An Imperfect Verifier is Good Enough: Learning with Noisy Rewards (2026)") §3.1, §5.1, §7). A bounded equivalence checker refuted a sizeable share of the rewards a result-match check paid in pilot text-to-SQL RL runs ([ReViSQL](#/papers/zhu2026revisql "Human-Level Text-to-SQL via Reinforcement Learning on Verified Data, Without Pipeline Engineering (2026)") §4.1). What a certificate buys is precision against systematic false accepts, not a lower error rate as such (ours).
- **Prompt-optimizer research needs such benchmarks** (see [Comparing LLM methods under noise](#/challenges/method_comparison_under_noise)): "cheap and deterministic to score, hard enough to leave room for improvement, and renewable as models evolve" ([Benchmarking Prompt Optimization of…](#/papers/lesort2026chess "Benchmarking Prompt Optimization of Large Language Models With Chess (2026)") abstract). Certified SQL pairs can meet all three needs, and a counterexample task needs no reference answer (ours); realistic hard pairs are [Sourcing realistic, hard query pairs](#/challenges/query_pair_sourcing)'s problem.
- **Where the gain shows up** (ours): [Checking text-to-SQL against intent](#/challenges/text_to_sql_verification), [Bringing compact models to frontier accuracy](#/challenges/compact_model_accuracy), [Weak checkers get exploited](#/challenges/weak_checker_exploitation) and the evaluation of [Query equivalence: prove or refute](#/challenges/query_equivalence) methods, where a weaker check can reorder methods ([SpotIt](#/papers/klopfenstein2025spotit "SpotIt: Evaluating Text-to-SQL Evaluation with Formal Verification (2026)") §5); certified *equivalent* labels depend on [SQL features that verifiers don't cover](#/challenges/verifier_coverage_gaps).

Why the ground truth can grow (our analogy, from the Source idea): AlphaGo Zero, learning by [self-play](#/glossary/self-play) from the rules alone, surpassed AlphaGo, which started from human games (Silver et al., *Nature* 2016 and 2017). Likewise, a benchmark need not rest on labels its builders fixed in advance when a program can check the answers: anyone can check a counterexample, so each one found, by any method, adds to the ground truth.

**Size of the gain:** step change for refutation labels, our estimate; on the accept side, certified *equivalent* labels are an enabler, bounded by [SQL features that verifiers don't cover](#/challenges/verifier_coverage_gaps) (ours). It is measured as the certified false-accept rate of today's labels and training rewards (a lower bound, from counterexamples validated by execution) and the change in scores and rankings once labels are certificates. The reason: a counterexample anyone can re-run behind every refutation, instead of a generator, a judge's tests or one database, makes one error class mostly disappear, credit for answers the check wrongly accepts, while a certificate of equivalence behind an accepted label reaches only as far as verifiers cover.

## Why it's hard
- Binary verdicts reward lucky guesses reached through faulty reasoning (ours): in EquiBench's case study a model classifies a pair correctly but misclassifies small buggy variants of it ([EquiBench](#/papers/wei2025equibench "EquiBench: Benchmarking Large Language Models' Reasoning about Program Semantics via Equivalence Checking (2025)") App. A.1).
- Labels saying "equivalent" come from rules, from one test database, or from failing to find a counterexample, and each can be wrong (see the examples above). Labels saying "not equivalent" on mutants often come from hand judgment ([Sourcing realistic, hard query pairs](#/challenges/query_pair_sourcing) § Why it's hard), and labels can also come from the method under test: most of GSM-HARD's answers come from rerunning PAL's own GSM8K programs with larger numbers, which, its authors note, assumes a program giving the right answer is itself correct ([PAL (Program-aided Language Models)](#/papers/gao2022pal "PAL: Program-aided Language Models (2022)") App.
- Some questions have several right queries, and one gold query marks the others wrong ([AmbiQT](#/papers/bhaskar2023ambiqt "Benchmarking and Improving Text-to-SQL Generation under Ambiguity (2023)"), [AMBROSIA](#/papers/saparina2024ambrosia "AMBROSIA: A Benchmark for Parsing Ambiguous Questions into Database Queries (2024)"); home: [Checking text-to-SQL against intent](#/challenges/text_to_sql_verification) § Why it's hard); [Pervasive Annotation Errors Break…](#/papers/jin2026annotation "Pervasive Annotation Errors Break Text-to-SQL Benchmarks and Leaderboards (2026)") counts ambiguity as an annotation error (§4.1, §4.4). A certificate settles whether two queries agree, not which reading was meant (ours; [The self-improving SQL ↔ text ↔ verify loop](#/challenges/sql_text_round_trip_loop) § Why it's hard). Proof checking shares the limit (ours): a Lean kernel certifies only that a proof establishes the formal statement, not that it matches the intended problem, note the authors of a benchmark audit; on unprovable flawed statements they corrected by hand, both provers tested went from solving none to solving some ([Faults in Our Formal Benchmarking](#/papers/ammanamanchi2026leandefects "Faults in Our Formal Benchmarking: Dataset Defects and Evaluation Failures in Lean Theorem Proving (2026)") §1, §4.3).

## Verifiable signal
Ask for counterexamples, not verdicts: each one found is verified and improves the ground truth for every method. A proof can certify the accept side (what counts: [Query equivalence: prove or refute](#/challenges/query_equivalence) § Verifiable signal; how often: [SQL features that verifiers don't cover](#/challenges/verifier_coverage_gaps)); pairs that stay *unknown* are the open frontier (§ What's known).
